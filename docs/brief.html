NijSetu: a context-aware privacy firewall for multilingual hospital records

Track 3 · Secret Wards · Members: <fill in: team member names> · Repo: https://github.com/Shreshtha280407/NijSetu

1. In one paragraph

NijSetu ingests CSV/TSV/JSON/FHIR R4/text exports from four fictional hospital systems — mixing English, Devanagari Hindi and Hinglish in the same sentence — strips every piece of personal data while keeping every medical value exactly intact, links the same patient across languages, files and hospitals with keyed-HMAC pseudonyms, and quantifies the privacy it bought: a real registry-based re-identification measurement ships with the release, not just a claim of “anonymised.”

2. Architecture

input/ (CSV/TSV/JSON/FHIR/TXT, 4 encodings)
  -> Ingestion        format/encoding detection; malformed files quarantined, never passed through
  -> Detection         rules + gazetteer + Devanagari transliteration + context scoring
                       (per script: latin / deva / hinglish)
  -> Identity linkage  blocking -> fuzzy/phonetic/cross-script match -> clustering;
                       evidence-gated: no shared strong ID or unique (name+clue) -> no link
  -> Privacy layer     per-patient HMAC date shift, age banding, geography generalisation,
                       small-cohort suppression
  -> Export            sanitized/, report.json, records.jsonl
         \-> Security layer wraps every raw/working artifact: AES-256-GCM at rest,
             fail-closed quarantine, PII-free logs, RAKSHA_SECRET never written

3. How each part works

  • Ingestion: format- and encoding-aware adapters (UTF-8 / UTF-8-BOM / UTF-16 / cp1252) for CSV/TSV/JSON/FHIR R4 Bundles/free text; anything truncated, malformed or unsupported is quarantined, never copied through half-cleaned.
  • Detection: layered rules + a bundled name/place gazetteer + Devanagari normalisation and transliteration + a scoring step that resolves the “meaning traps” (C/O <relative> vs. C/O <complaint>, Dr. Ramesh vs. relative Ramesh, 1-0-1 dose vs. phone/ID) rather than one script-blind regex pass.
  • Linkage & pseudonyms: blocking keys plus fuzzy/phonetic/cross-script comparison cluster records into patients; a cluster forms only on a shared strong identifier or a (name + second clue) combination unique in the dataset — ambiguous evidence is left unlinked by design, since a false merge is scored worse than a missed link. IDs are issued via keyed HMAC (RAKSHA_SECRET), never plain hashing.
  • Privacy layer: date shifts are per-patient (HMAC-keyed, never a global offset — a global shift protects nobody once two dates are recovered), age is banded, geography is generalised (village/house/landmark suppressed, district retained), and small cohorts below the k-threshold escalate to coarser bands automatically.
  • Security: AES-256-GCM (the cryptography package) protects NijSetu’s own raw/working files at rest — the dashboard’s uploaded files and the CLI’s private analysis artifact — each under a fresh in-memory key, domain-separated from the HMAC pseudonym key. Decrypted bytes are staged only in a directory verified (against /proc/mounts, not assumed) to be tmpfs, never on persistent disk. Every error is a reason code, never a raw traceback or the secret.

4. USP

  • Cross-script identity matching, not exact-string lookups: the same patient is linked as “Mohammad Arif” / “मोहम्मद आरिफ़” / “Md. Arif” / “Arif sahab” through transliteration-aware blocking, not a name list.
  • Context traps handled explicitly — relative vs. doctor, complaint vs. care-of, dose vs. phone/ID — instead of one regex pass that cannot tell them apart.
  • Measured, not assumed, privacy–utility trade-offs: a repeatable harness (scripts/policy_tradeoff.py) reruns the real pipeline under each policy knob and reports what it costs in clinical utility and what it buys in quasi-identifier uniqueness against an actual registry (§5).
  • Tested for generalisation, not just the practice set: an independently authored corpus sharing no surface forms with the practice data (new hospitals, header spellings, ID prefixes, encodings) is scored against its own answer key.

The shipped “balanced” policy (10-year age bands, district retained, ±30-day per-patient date shift) keeps 97.3% of clinical utility while leaving only 4 of 788 released records singly-identifiable against the registry on quasi-identifiers alone — the residual is the Re-identification Trap: rare (district, age-band, gender) combinations that survive even after date shifting. Suppressing geography entirely removes those 4–5 records but is not the shipped default, since it is a district-wide utility cost paid to fix a handful of rare rows; the honest alternative — suppressing only the rare cohorts — is exactly the k-threshold escalation NijSetu already runs, and §6 states where it still falls short.

6. Known limitations

  • Linking recall is 0.74 by design. Roughly a quarter of truly-linkable records are left unlinked when the evidence (name + one clue) is not unique in the dataset, because a missed link costs less than a false merge. Some genuinely-same-patient records across languages will therefore surface under separate pseudonyms rather than be wrongly merged.
  • A handful of rare cohorts remain singly-identifiable. The automatic small-cohort escalation (k-threshold = 3) did not fully close the gap the external registry measurement found (§5): 4 of 788 released records still land in a unique (district, age-band, gender) cohort. Closing it needs suppression of geography on those specific rare rows, not a dataset-wide policy change.
  • Practice-tuned rules lose ground on genuinely novel formats. The organizer’s own calibration table shows even a strong reference implementation loses real points on the unseen-format round; NijSetu’s heldout-corpus test (0/176 leaks) is independent evidence of generalisation, not proof the hidden “never seen” round will score identically.
  • This brief’s measurements are practice-only. They come from dev/input (200 files, 48 patients) with the organizer’s own check_dev.py and this repo’s policy_tradeoff.py against a 240-person registry — not the hidden evaluation datasets, which are larger and have new patients.
